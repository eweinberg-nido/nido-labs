How levels are recorded

Each sub-skill is assessed on its own. A student's level in one sub-skill does not depend on their level in another, and there is no expectation that levels will be even across a domain.

  • Half points describe partial attainment of the next level. A student with a 2.5 can do what 2.0 describes consistently, and can do some of what 3.0 describes, usually in one piece of work rather than throughout. A student with a 3.5 can do what 3.0 describes consistently, and some of what 4.0 describes, often in a single strand of a project rather than across all of it.
  • A half point is not a reward for effort, for the volume of work submitted, or for improvement considered on its own. It records that part of the next level is present in the evidence.
  • Where a student has not yet produced evidence, no level is recorded. The portfolio shows that the evidence is not there yet, which is more useful to both the student and the advisor than a low level would be.

Reading a portfolio

A suggested order of reading, intended to make judgements quicker to reach and easier to explain.

  • Read the growth ledger before the curated work. A finished artifact shows what a student can produce. The ledger shows how they got there, which is what most of these sub-skills describe.
  • Look for an entry where something was set aside or went backwards. A ledger of steady improvement is often written from memory at the end. A record made as the work happened usually contains a dead end.
  • Consider what prompted each change. Changes made because a teacher asked sit around 2.0. Changes the student initiated sit around 3.0. Changes forced by the student's own evidence sit at 4.0.
  • A comment such as "people said it looks good" is not evidence of process. It describes a reaction to finished work, and it is the most frequent reason a sub-skill is proposed at 3.0 when the evidence supports 2.0.
  • Reach your own judgement before reading the level the student proposed. Recording a different level is ordinary, and between them these two portfolios contain four such adjustments, one of which is upward.
  • Each verification should end with a single next step. If a next step cannot be written, there is usually not yet enough evidence to record a level, and saying so is itself useful feedback.
Notes on how these exemplars were chosen
  • Neither portfolio is uniform. Exemplar A includes a 2.0, proposed by the student herself. Levels vary within a strong portfolio, and a calibration set drawn only from top-level work encourages students to imitate the descriptors rather than to document their own learning.
  • Exemplar B is not the portfolio of a student who has disengaged. Tomás cares about his project, and his strongest piece of evidence is better than what many higher-scoring students hold in that sub-skill. An exemplar that looked careless would misrepresent the students colleagues will actually be assessing.
  • Both portfolios are unevenly filled, and Exemplar B has a show date with nothing recorded at all. The template needs to look normal when it is half complete, because many of them will be.
  • Exemplar B is the stronger in one sub-skill. If the exemplars never cross over in this way, there is a risk of levelling the student rather than the evidence.
